**Foundational Models and Architectures for Low‑Hallucination Financial & Tabular Market‑Intelligence Systems (2024–2026)**

**Executive overview**

Recent work on financial LLMs, tabular‑data LLMs, and hallucination benchmarks converges on a few patterns for building high‑stakes market‑intelligence systems.

First, instead of relying on a single fine‑tuned model, successful systems combine a strong general or finance‑specialized open‑source base model with retrieval (RAG/GraphRAG) plus explicit guardrails and numeric cross‑checks.[1][2][3][4]

Second, fine‑tuning is usually applied to schemas, instructions, and reasoning style rather than baking volatile numerical facts into weights, especially when live APIs and frequently updated PDFs are involved.[5][6][7][1]

Third, for structured/tabular and financial data, the most promising open‑source backbones include finance‑tuned models such as FinGPT, table‑specialized models such as TableLLM, and high‑end general models (DeepSeek, Qwen‑3 series, etc.) that score well on financial/tabular benchmarks, typically wrapped in retrieval and guardrail layers.[8][9][10][11][12][13]

The sections below synthesize current findings to answer three questions:

1. Which open‑source base models are best suited for structured financial and multi‑tabular workloads?
2. What open‑source frameworks/methodologies exist to suppress hallucinations in high‑stakes financial/business reporting?
3. For heterogeneous civic, budget, and market datasets, what is the emerging consensus: full‑data fine‑tuning vs schema‑only tuning plus RAG/GraphRAG?

**1. Open‑source base models for structured financial and tabular data**

**1.1 Finance‑specialized LLMs (FinGPT family)**

The FinGPT project from AI4Finance provides an open‑source framework and checkpoints specifically tuned for finance, with a data‑centric pipeline and support for lightweight adaptation methods such as LoRA.[12][14][^13]

The FinGPT ecosystem explicitly addresses the temporal sensitivity of financial data and offers components for real‑time NLP, instruction tuning, RAG, and benchmarking on financial tasks.[14][13][^12]

Because your application is market‑intelligence oriented, FinGPT (or using its training recipes on a stronger 2025–2026 backbone) is a strong candidate as a “finance‑aware” base that understands reports, filings, and common financial terminology.

Key properties relevant to your use case:

- Data‑centric design with automatic data curation pipelines for financial news, filings, and social media, designed to be updated frequently.[15][13]
- LLM layer built around lightweight adaptation (LoRA/QLoRA) for cost‑effective continual updates.[13][12]
- Existing FinGPT‑RAG component showing how to combine a financial LLM with retrieval for sentiment and analytics tasks, which parallels your hybrid static‑PDF + live‑API setting.[12][14]

**1.2 Tabular‑specialized LLMs (TableLLM and related work)**

A 2024–2025 line of work focuses on “Tabular LLMs” designed explicitly for table understanding and manipulation, surveyed in a dedicated GitHub tutorial and multiple papers.[2][11][^16]

TableLLM (TableLLM‑8B), built on top of Llama‑3.1‑8B, is fine‑tuned to handle operations over spreadsheet‑like tables and achieves state‑of‑the‑art or near‑SOTA performance on several table QA benchmarks such as WikiSQL and WikiTQ.[^10]

The TableLLM repository notes that TableLLM‑8B “is designed to handle tabular data manipulation tasks efficiently… in real office usage scenarios,” and the benchmark table shows significantly higher average accuracy than popular baselines across multiple datasets.[^10]

Combined with the broader SIGIR’24 tutorial on LLMs for tabular data, this suggests specialized tabular LLMs are a strong fit for tasks like cross‑sheet joins, aggregation, and schema‑aware reasoning, which resemble your multi‑tabular civic and budget datasets.[11][2]

**1.3 High‑end general open‑source LLMs with strong financial performance**

Industry guides and benchmarks for 2025–2026 consistently highlight new open‑source families (DeepSeek‑R1, Qwen‑3 series, and similar) as top general‑purpose models for reasoning and financial analysis.[17][18][^8]

A 2026 “best LLM for finance” guide selects DeepSeek‑R1, Qwen3‑235B‑A22B, and QwQ‑32B as the strongest open‑source options for financial analysis and quantitative tasks, citing their reasoning and mathematical precision.[^8]

Broader 2025 reviews of open‑source LLMs also place Qwen and Mistral/Mixtral families near the top of open‑source leaderboards for general performance, with good coding and reasoning ability that transfers to financial tooling.[19][17]

For a city‑scale Indian market‑intelligence use case, these larger general models offer:

- Strong base reasoning, which is important when composing civic indicators, budgets, and market data into feasibility narratives.
- Better multilingual capabilities (e.g., Qwen‑2.5/3’s coverage of 30+ languages) which can help with regional Indian contexts.[^19]
- Flexibility to fine‑tune or instruct‑tune on local schemas, without being locked into finance‑only corpora.

**1.4 Recommended “top three” families for your stack**

Based on current evidence, three model families stand out as strong candidates for your specific goal (structured financial + civic/tabular data with low hallucination tolerance):

| Role in system | Candidate model family | Rationale |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Finance‑aware text and filings understanding | **FinGPT** (framework plus checkpoints) | Financially tuned, data‑centric, supports LoRA, includes RAG component and benchmarks specifically for financial NLP.[12][14][^13] |
| Tabular reasoning over civic, budget, and rental tables | **TableLLM (TableLLM‑8B)** | Purpose‑built for tabular manipulation in “real office” scenarios, SOTA‑level performance on table QA; based on Llama‑3.1.[10][11] |
| High‑capacity general reasoning + multilingual | **DeepSeek‑R1 / Qwen‑3‑series** | Rated among top open‑source models for finance and quantitative reasoning; strong math and reasoning benchmarks, plus multilingual support.[8][17][^18] |

In practice, many teams use a single general model with appropriate RAG, but for a highly critical “zero‑hallucination” system, it is reasonable to:

- Use a strong general model (e.g., Qwen‑3‑72B or DeepSeek‑R1) as the main conversational backbone.
- Attach a specialized tabular tool model (TableLLM) via tool calling for heavy spreadsheet‑like queries.
- Borrow FinGPT’s data‑engineering and fine‑tuning recipes (or its checkpoints) to bias the backbone toward financial language and tasks.

**2. Open‑source frameworks and methodologies for hallucination control in finance**

**2.1 Financial hallucination benchmarks and evaluation frameworks**

Several recent academic and industry works provide explicit benchmarks for hallucinations in financial and tabular contexts:

- **FAITH (Framework for Assessing Intrinsic Tabular Hallucinations in Finance)**: A 2025 framework targeting intrinsic hallucinations on financial tables by masking numeric spans in S&P 500 annual report tables and asking LLMs to recover them, with strict criteria for uniqueness, consistency, and answerability.[20][21][^22]
- **FailSafeQA**: A financial QA benchmark to test robustness, hallucinations, and compliance on 10‑K filings, focusing on accuracy, refusals when data is missing, and how models behave under OCR noise and domain shifts.[^23]
- Other works such as PHANTOM (hallucination detection in long‑context financial QA) further characterize hallucination patterns over long documents.[^24]

While these are benchmarks rather than end‑user frameworks, they provide:

- Datasets you can reuse to evaluate your chosen base models and tuning strategies.
- Task designs (e.g., masked numeric prediction, long‑context QA) you can adapt to your own civic and Indian budget data to stress‑test numerical faithfulness.

**2.2 General hallucination‑mitigation surveys and method taxonomies**

A 2024 survey, “A Comprehensive Survey of Hallucination Mitigation Techniques in Large Language Models,” categorizes mitigation strategies into prompt engineering, retrieval‑augmented generation (before/during/after generation), self‑refinement with feedback, decoding strategies, knowledge‑graph utilization, faithfulness‑based loss functions, and supervised fine‑tuning.[^3]

A 2026 framework paper on hallucination management in LLMs proposes an operational, multi‑tier architecture that combines uncertainty estimation, context grounding, confidence calibration, and feedback loops, illustrated with a financial data extraction case study.[^25]

An “Awesome‑Hallucination‑Detection‑and‑Mitigation” GitHub list maintains up‑to‑date resources on benchmarks like RAGTruth and methods such as knowledge‑based factual checking.[^26]

Taken together, these indicate that hallucination control is best treated as a system‑level problem, not just a model fine‑tuning issue.

**2.3 RAG and GraphRAG frameworks tailored to financial data**

Retrieval‑augmented generation (RAG) is a core technique for reducing hallucinations by forcing the model to ground answers in retrieved documents.[6][7][1][5]

For finance specifically, there is growing interest in GraphRAG, where a knowledge graph is built over financial data (e.g., entities from annual reports), then combined with vector retrieval:

- Microsoft Research’s **GraphRAG** project constructs a knowledge graph over private narrative data, then uses it during retrieval to provide richer, more structured context to LLMs.[27][28][^29]
- A financial “Hybrid‑Graph‑RAG‑Financial‑Analyser” GitHub repo shows a concrete implementation using Neo4j and LangChain to analyse Apple’s 2024 annual report by combining graph‑based retrieval with vector search.[^30]
- Multiple blog posts and curated lists (e.g., “Awesome‑GraphRAG”) highlight GraphRAG’s benefits for accuracy, explainability, and governance in financial services.[31][32][^33]

This line of work supports the idea that for your civic and financial datasets (budgets, schemes, rentals, etc.), GraphRAG is particularly useful to:

- Encode entities such as cities, wards, schemes, and projects as nodes with typed relationships (e.g., “project‑uses‑scheme”, “ward‑has‑dam”, “market‑has‑rental‑rate”).
- Enforce that responses are derived from walks over this graph plus attached source documents, which improves traceability and explainability.

**2.4 Guardrail and provenance frameworks**

Several open‑source or open‑spec frameworks explicitly target hallucination control by validating outputs against reference data:

- **Guardrails AI** provides validators for hallucination detection based on provenance, including a Wikipedia‑based validator and a more general approach that checks whether claims in the output are supported by a reference knowledge base.[34][35][^26]
- **OpenAI Guardrails (Python/TS)** exposes a “Hallucination Detection” check that validates claims against a vector store of reference documents and flags unsupported or contradicted content, with configurable confidence thresholds.[36][37]
- Industry blogs on AI guardrails for financial services emphasize techniques such as range checks for numeric outputs, reconciliation with official filings, and mandatory citation of data sources, which can dramatically reduce ungrounded financial claims.[38][39][^4]

In your context, a practical pattern is:

- Use RAG/GraphRAG to fetch the relevant budget/rental/civic slices.
- Require the model to output structured JSON with fields for each numeric figure and its source reference (table ID, row/column, API endpoint, timestamp).
- Run a guardrail layer that verifies each figure against the source (or at least checks consistency and plausible ranges) before returning it to the user.

**2.5 Finance‑specific LLM frameworks with hallucination‑aware components**

FinGPT’s framework includes an explicit RAG variant (FinGPT‑RAG) that focuses on grounding sentiment analysis and prediction in retrieved financial documents.[14][12]

Practical guides for LLMs in finance from institutions like CFA Institute emphasize the need for RLHF, strong evaluation, and guardrails to manage hallucinations because finance is highly technical and context‑dependent.[^40]

Benchmarks like FailSafeQA and FAITH, together with industry evaluations (e.g., hallucination benchmarks of leading models in 2026), show that even state‑of‑the‑art models will fabricate numbers when data is incomplete, reinforcing the need for external validation.[41][18][21][23]

These results strongly support building your system as a multi‑component pipeline rather than betting on a single “zero‑hallucination” model.

**3. Fine‑tuning vs schema‑only tuning plus RAG/GraphRAG**

**3.1 General fine‑tuning vs RAG consensus**

Multiple contemporary guides from cloud providers and knowledge‑management vendors compare fine‑tuning against RAG along a common set of dimensions.

A 2025 article from Memgraph notes that fine‑tuning is suited to static/repetitive tasks but is expensive, slow to update, and less flexible, while RAG is lightweight, dynamic, and better for real‑time information needs.[^1]

A 2026 guide from Atlan similarly contrasts fine‑tuning (“static, baked‑in knowledge,” high update cost) with RAG (“dynamic, reflects updates instantly,” lower update cost but some extra latency).

It recommends RAG or a hybrid approach when data freshness, auditability, and heterogeneity are key.[^5]

Oracle and Glean both argue that for many enterprise applications, the best practice is to fine‑tune models for domain understanding and style, then use RAG to inject up‑to‑date, company‑specific facts at inference time.[7][6]

This aligns with the broader consensus that:

- Volatile numerical data (prices, budgets, rainfall levels, dam storage) should not be baked into weights.
- Fine‑tuning is most valuable for teaching the model domain schemas, jargon, and preferred reasoning patterns.

**3.2 Tabular‑data specific considerations**

A 2024 survey “Large Language Models on Tabular Data” points out that LLMs struggle with precise tabular reasoning and that specialized encodings, decomposition strategies, or hybrid systems are often needed.[^2]

Tutorials on LLMs for tabular data at SIGIR’24 emphasize that table QA systems increasingly decompose tasks into retrieval, symbolic computation (e.g., SQL), and verification, rather than relying on the LLM to directly predict numbers from raw tables.[16][11]

Community experience from practitioners (e.g., LocalLLaMA discussions) also notes that vanilla LLMs often make mistakes even on small CSVs, and recommend programmatic extraction plus targeted LLM reasoning, or graph‑based retrieval, for robust results.[^42]

Given your heavy use of multi‑tabular civic and budget data, these observations suggest:

- Use the LLM as a planner and explainer, not as the primary numerical calculator.
- Keep computations in explicit code (SQL, Pandas, or specialized numerical engines) with the LLM orchestrating and describing the results.

**3.3 GraphRAG in heterogeneous civic and market datasets**

GraphRAG is particularly attractive for datasets that mix structured entities (cities, wards, schemes, schools, dams) with semi‑structured or narrative data (scheme descriptions, election manifestos, market reports).

Microsoft’s GraphRAG work shows how a knowledge graph built from private narrative data enables deeper discovery and more grounded answers.[^29]

Financial‑sector and banking blogs argue that GraphRAG improves accuracy, explainability, and governance in domains like risk and fraud by making relationships explicit and traceable.[43][33][^31]

Curated “Awesome‑GraphRAG” lists track this emerging ecosystem and highlight its role in overcoming traditional RAG limitations through structured graphs, multi‑hop reasoning, and better context selection.[32][28]

For your setting (Indian civic indicators, state budgets, commercial rentals, competitors):

- Graph nodes could represent locations, sectors, schemes, infrastructure projects, rental markets, and competitor entities.
- Properties store numeric metrics (e.g., budget allocations, rainfall, dam levels, rents), with edges capturing relationships (e.g., “scheme‑funds‑project”, “project‑located‑in‑ward”, “ward‑belongs‑to‑city”).
- RAG then retrieves relevant subgraphs (plus attached document snippets and tables) that the LLM uses to generate feasibility reports with explicit grounding.

**3.4 Emerging consensus for your type of dataset**

From both academic surveys and enterprise guidance, a consistent pattern emerges for complex, heterogeneous, and fast‑changing data:

- **Do not** fine‑tune the LLM on all raw data (tables, PDFs, APIs) with the goal of encoding every fact.
    - This is expensive, risks staleness, and does not reliably eliminate hallucinations.[1][5][^2]
- **Do** fine‑tune or instruct‑tune the LLM on:
    - Data schemas (column names, relationships between tables, allowed joins).
    - Task formats (e.g., “business feasibility report” templates, reasoning chains, refusal behavior when data is missing).
    - Domain language and examples of good versus bad reports grounded in evidence.
- **Use RAG/GraphRAG + tools to fetch and compute actual numbers at inference time**, including:
    - Vector/graph retrieval over state budgets, civic datasets, and PDFs.
    - Programmatic access to live APIs (civic data portals, rental sites, crop prices) to fill numerical fields.
    - Dedicated tabular agents (TableLLM or SQL/text‑to‑SQL pipelines) for complex aggregations.
- **Layer guardrails and evaluators on top** to check that each numeric claim can be traced back to a source and that missing data triggers explicit “unknown / cannot determine” statements.

This “schema‑tuning + GraphRAG + numeric tooling + guardrails” pattern is increasingly seen as the safest architecture for high‑stakes financial and civic applications where hallucinations are unacceptable.[21][6][7][3][20][25][5][1]

**4. Concrete design recommendations for your system**

**4.1 Recommended model and training strategy**

- Choose a strong general open‑source base (e.g., Qwen‑3‑72B or DeepSeek‑R1) and optionally distill to a smaller on‑prem model if latency/cost require it.[18][17][^8]
- Integrate TableLLM‑style capabilities for heavy tabular reasoning, either by:
    - Using TableLLM‑8B directly as a tool model, or
    - Adapting TableLLM’s training recipe to your own Llama‑3‑based checkpoint with your civic/budget tables.[11][10]
- Use FinGPT’s pipeline as a template for ingesting and cleaning financial‑like data (budgets, revenue, civic spending) and for performing lightweight LoRA/QLoRA fine‑tuning on:
    - Schema understanding (how tables relate, what each field means).
    - Output formatting (feasibility report sections, risk flags, justifications).
    - Refusal patterns when data is incomplete.

Avoid full‑scale fine‑tuning on raw numeric values; instead, fine‑tune on synthetic tasks that teach the model to call tools, interpret schemas, and ground claims in retrieved evidence.

**4.2 Retrieval and graph layer**

- Build a knowledge graph over your civic and market data using tools like Neo4j, following GraphRAG patterns.[30][27][32][29]
- Attach vector indices to nodes and documents (PDFs of budgets, tenancy contracts, policy documents) so both structural and semantic retrieval are available.
- Implement hybrid retrieval where:
    - Graph queries identify relevant entities and relationships (e.g., all schemes affecting a ward, all rental listings in a micro‑market).
    - Vector RAG fetches detailed text and table snippets.

**4.3 Hallucination‑control mechanisms**

- Use financial hallucination benchmarks (FAITH, FailSafeQA, PHANTOM) as part of your evaluation to choose base models and measure improvements.[23][20][21][24]
- Implement guardrails/provenance checks:
    - Require each numeric statement to be labeled with a source ID and timestamp.
    - Cross‑check against your graph or warehouse; reject or flag responses with unsupported numbers.[4][37][38][26][^34]
- Consider uncertainty‑aware decoding and self‑refinement techniques from the hallucination‑mitigation survey (e.g., ask the model to generate and critique its own reasoning, and to explicitly declare “insufficient data” when appropriate).[3][25]

**4.4 Developer ergonomics and evaluation**

- Maintain a library of canonical feasibility‑report exemplars annotated with: correct numbers, allowed evidence sources, and rationales.
- Use these exemplars to train both the model (via SFT/RL‑style preference data) and the guardrail/evaluation layer.
- Continuously monitor:
    - Hallucination rate (unsupported numbers per report).
    - Refusal behavior (how often the system appropriately says it cannot answer).
    - Latency/throughput given your budget and on‑premise constraints.
